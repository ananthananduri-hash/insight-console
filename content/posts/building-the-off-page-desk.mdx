Off-page SEO looks simple on paper: earn links, claim listings you already deserve, win back the ones that broke. In practice, for a large client with multiple business lines, it fell apart constantly — not because the work itself was hard, but because almost every single action depended on someone outside my control.

## Where it actually broke

A single link placement could need a client approver, a product owner, a web team, and an external publisher to all reply — each on their own schedule. Three genuinely different kinds of work (earning, claiming, reclaiming) got treated like one undifferentiated backlog. And the part that stung most: follow-ups lived in memory. Who got chased, when, and what they promised sat scattered across inboxes and personal notes, so items stalled quietly with nobody noticing until someone asked where things stood.

Reporting made it worse. The end-of-day update and the monthly continuity report were rebuilt by hand from email threads every single time, and a link that quietly went live was easy to miss entirely. Worse than that — once something *did* go live, there was no routine way to check it stayed live, or to tell whether it actually moved a ranking.

## What I built instead

The Off-Page Desk is one workspace, one shared database, with tabs that each answer a question the work actually asks every day: what's due today, what can we claim, what can we reclaim, who links to us and what changed, are the links we won still live, and did a live link move a ranking.

Three decisions mattered more than the tool itself:

- **Every item lives in Earn, Claim, or Reclaim** — not just "to do." Each one carries a stage, a next step, a next date, and a running record of what's happened, so nothing sits ownerless.
- **Follow-up cadences are rules, not reminders.** Client items get chased every three working days and escalate to the agency manager after two chases go unanswered. Publisher items get five working days and close out after two. Nobody has to remember to chase — the system surfaces it.
- **Scans feed the pipeline, not guesswork.** Backlink data gets triaged automatically into claim and reclaim candidates, so the team starts from evidence instead of manually hunting for opportunities. One of these scans surfaced a public encyclopedia reference that pointed at the wrong company website entirely — fixed through the platform's own conflict-of-interest disclosure process once we verified it.

## The one rule I didn't automate on purpose

The Desk drafts the end-of-day update from the day's recorded activity — links live, placements, approvals. It never sends anything. A person reviews and sends every outbound message. That's a deliberate ceiling on the automation, not a missing feature: it's the reason the client trusts the speed instead of being wary of it.

## What changed, measurably

The first effect was simply control: at last count, every open item — all of them — had a named owner, a next step, and a date, a thing that used to be unknowable without reconstructing it from threads.

A few real numbers off the Desk's own Reports tab, from one recent reporting month:

| Measure | What the Desk showed |
|---|---|
| Open items with an owner and a next date | 100% (every open item) |
| Items moved forward in the month | 22 |
| Follow-ups sent | 52, across 24 items |
| New items opened / closed | 9 opened, 17 closed |
| Links confirmed live in the month | 7 |
| Tracked won links still live (no manual nudging) | 9 of 17 — the rest are either still awaiting placement or need a manual check because the page blocks automated checks |
| Lost or broken backlinks triaged in one reclaim scan | 47 checked, 34 filtered out as spam or low-value before anyone spent time on them, 0 false positives |
| New genuine referring domains found in 30 days | 4 |
| Links with before/after traffic tracked | 2 tracked, 2 measured, 1 already showing more sessions to the page it points to |

None of these are vanity metrics — they're the same numbers the Desk surfaces to me every morning. The ones I'd still call early (ranking movement specifically, since it needs a longer baseline) I'm leaving as "still collecting" rather than rounding up.

## What I'd tell someone building something similar

Organize around who you're waiting on, not around what you're doing — most of the delay in this kind of work isn't yours to control, so your system should make the waiting visible instead of hiding it. Rules beat reminders, every time, because a fixed cadence removes the argument about *when* to follow up. And verify before you act: a scan that hands you unverified "opportunities" just moves the wasted time earlier in the process instead of removing it.

## Update: from an activity tracker to a strategy-aware system

I had a colleague who works in off-page SEO review the Desk once it had been running a while, and the critique was fair: it tracks activity well — everything owned, chased, and logged — but nothing in it said whether a link was worth earning in the first place. A system that's busy isn't the same as a system that's working. Five changes came out of that review:

- **A quality gate before any effort goes in.** Every opportunity now gets scored — topical relevance to the business line, the outlet's real traffic, follow status, and where on the page the link sits — before it becomes a tracked item. Anything below the floor gets parked instead of worked. Third-party authority scores are a weak proxy for any of this; they're a guess at a guess, so they're weighted below relevance and real traffic, not above them.
- **Target pages and anchor text are planned, not default.** Every item now names the actual page the link should point to — a service page that sells, not the homepage — and an anchor type, kept mostly branded or partial-match. An all-exact-match link profile is the fastest way to look manufactured instead of earned.
- **Earning needs something worth linking to, not just outreach.** Cold pitching plateaus. The highest-leverage addition is tracking original research and data assets — the kind of proprietary numbers a journalist can cite without having to take a brand's word for anything — on a separate campaign timeline, paired with a bench of named internal experts available for quotes.
- **Reputation lives beyond links.** Analyst and peer-review presence, industry-body listings, and consistent identity records (Wikipedia, Wikidata, Crunchbase, organization schema) all feed the same credibility signal links do — and increasingly, they're also what AI answer engines cite directly. That's now tracked on its own quarterly cadence instead of competing for attention with daily chases.
- **Outcomes get reported, not just activity.** Pitches sent and links won are the funnel, not the result. The monthly report now tracks quality referring domains gained against a competitor set, link retention, share of voice, AI-answer citations, and referral sessions to the pages that actually sell — because sessions alone don't prove a program is working.

The one thing that mattered most wasn't a new tracking field at all: most of what stalled the Desk was waiting on approvals, so the highest-leverage fix was organizational — standing pre-approval for defined categories, instead of one more clever automation.

The same "activity isn't strategy" lesson turned out to apply at a much smaller scale too — [I made the same upgrade to two personal Notion tools](/blog/execution-isnt-strategy-pantrybuddy-pumpdude), a grocery price tracker and a fuel mileage log, neither of which had anything to do with SEO.
