I keep a short list of stories I come back to when I want to think clearly about consent. Henrietta Lacks is at the top of it, and it's not because the science is remarkable — it is — but because the story refuses to let "informed consent" stay an abstraction. It makes you sit with a very specific, very uncomfortable question: *what happens to a piece of you after you've stopped being able to say yes or no to how it's used?*

## The part most retellings undersell

Here's the detail that sticks with me most: Henrietta Lacks' cells were taken in 1951, during treatment for cervical cancer at Johns Hopkins, without her knowledge. That part gets mentioned in almost every version of this story. What gets glossed over is *how long the asymmetry lasted*. Her family didn't find out her cells had been taken — let alone that they'd become one of the most-used tools in modern biomedical research — for nearly sixty years. Six decades where one side of that relationship had full knowledge and the other had none.

Sixty years is longer than most careers. It's long enough for an entire field of bioethics to be built, for HIPAA to be written into law, for "informed consent" to become a standard phrase in every clinical trial on earth — and still, the specific people this happened to were the last to know.

That gap is the whole lesson, for me. Not the cells. The gap.

## Why the science doesn't let you look away

The reason this story has staying power instead of fading into a historical footnote is that the thing taken from her turned out to matter *enormously*. Her cells, nicknamed "HeLa," didn't behave like the cell samples researchers were used to — most died within a day outside the body; hers kept dividing, doubling roughly every 20 to 24 hours, indefinitely. That one property is the reason HeLa cells ended up inside the development of the polio vaccine, decades of cancer and HIV research, and — much later — COVID-19 vaccine research. They've even been flown into orbit to study how living cells behave in zero gravity.

If the cells had been unremarkable, this might have stayed a private medical footnote. Because they weren't, the ethical question got dragged into the light on a scale nobody could ignore: a single, nonconsensual act became load-bearing infrastructure for essentially all of modern medicine.

## The question I actually sit with

I don't think the useful takeaway here is "medical research was shadier in 1951, good thing we've fixed it." The specific legal gaps have mostly closed. The underlying tension hasn't.

Every time I set up tracking on this blog — deciding what counts as a meaningful event, what gets stored, what a visitor has actually agreed to versus what I could technically collect — I think about the sixty-year gap. Not because cookie consent and cervical cancer treatment are morally equivalent; they obviously aren't. But because the same structural question sits underneath both: *does the person the data came from know what's happening to it, in terms they'd actually recognize as true?*

That's a harder bar than "did we technically disclose this somewhere." Henrietta Lacks' doctors could probably have pointed to the consent norms of 1951 and called it compliant. It still wasn't right, and it still took sixty years for the people most affected to find out. Compliance and honesty aren't the same test, and the gap between them is usually where the real damage happens — whether the asymmetry runs for sixty years or just exists for sixty milliseconds, in the moment between a page loading and a visitor realizing what just got recorded about them.

I don't have a clean ending for this one. I just notice I keep reaching for Henrietta Lacks' name whenever "consent" starts to feel like a checkbox instead of a question.
