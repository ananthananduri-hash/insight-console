In January 1951, a woman named Henrietta Lacks went to Johns Hopkins Hospital — one of the few hospitals in Baltimore at the time that treated Black patients — with pain and bleeding she couldn't explain. She was diagnosed with cervical cancer. She died later that year, at 31, leaving behind five children.

What happened next, without her knowledge or consent, changed medicine forever.

## The birth of HeLa cells

During her treatment, a sample of Henrietta's cancer cells was sent to Dr. George Gey's tissue lab at Johns Hopkins. Gey was an experienced cancer researcher, but even he wasn't prepared for what he found: while most cell samples died within 24 hours outside the body, Henrietta's cells didn't just survive — they doubled every 20 to 24 hours.

Gey developed a technique for growing them at scale and named the line "HeLa," using the first two letters of her first and last name.

Those cells never stopped dividing. They're still dividing today, in labs around the world.

## What HeLa cells made possible

The scale of HeLa's contribution to medicine is hard to overstate:

- The **polio vaccine** was developed and tested using HeLa cells
- They've been central to **COVID-19 vaccine research**
- They enabled decades of study into **leukemia, HIV, and cancer** more broadly
- They were sent into orbit on the Soviet satellite Sputnik-6 and on human spaceflights, to study how living cells behave in zero gravity

A sample taken from one woman, without her knowledge, became one of the most important tools in the history of medical research.

## The part of the story that took decades to tell

For nearly 60 years, Henrietta Lacks' family didn't know her cells had been taken, let alone that they'd become a multi-billion-dollar research and commercial resource. It wasn't until Rebecca Skloot's 2010 book, *The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks*, that the full story — and the ethical questions it raises — reached a wide audience. The book's success pushed Johns Hopkins to publicly reckon with its history and its ongoing relationship with the Lacks family.

In recent years, Henrietta Lacks has been posthumously honored by the WHO, commemorated with a statue at the University of Bristol, and recognized with a building at Johns Hopkins bearing her name.

## Why this still matters for healthcare data today

Henrietta Lacks' story predates modern data privacy law by decades, but the core question it raises hasn't gone away: who owns the data (biological or digital) that a patient's body or behavior generates, and what do they deserve to know about how it's used?

Every healthcare organization running analytics today — tracking patient journeys, de-identifying records for research, building marketing funnels around health services — is operating in the shadow of this exact question. Consent, transparency, and de-identification aren't just compliance checkboxes; they're the modern answer to a question Henrietta Lacks' family had to wait sixty years to even ask.
